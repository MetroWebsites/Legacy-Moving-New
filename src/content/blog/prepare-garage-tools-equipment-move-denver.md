## TL;DR

Moving a garage requires careful planning since it's usually the most cluttered space in any home. Start by sorting tools and equipment into keep, donate, and discard piles at least three weeks before your move. Drain all fluids from power equipment, secure loose parts with zip ties or tape, and pack hand tools in their original cases when possible. Use heavy-duty boxes for small items, clearly label everything as "garage" with room destination, and take photos of complex tool setups before disassembling. Hazardous materials like paint, chemicals, and propane tanks cannot go on the moving truck—dispose of them properly or transport them yourself. The Denver metro area has specific disposal sites for hazardous waste, and professional movers can help with the heavy lifting but won't transport flammable or combustible materials.

If you've ever opened your garage door and wondered how on earth you accumulated so much stuff, you're not alone. The garage is where tools multiply, half-empty paint cans congregate, and that broken lawn mower you've been meaning to fix for two years sits gathering dust. When it comes time to move, the garage often becomes the most overwhelming room in the house—not because the items are particularly valuable, but because they're bulky, oddly shaped, and sometimes downright hazardous.

Moving a garage full of tools and equipment in the Denver area comes with its own unique challenges. Between altitude considerations for equipment that might need recalibration, seasonal weather that affects what you can safely transport, and local regulations about hazardous materials disposal, there's more to think about than just throwing everything in boxes. Whether you're moving from a house in Lakewood to an apartment in downtown Denver, or relocating your entire workshop to a new property in Highlands Ranch, this guide will walk you through exactly how to prepare your garage for moving day.

## Start With a Ruthless Inventory Three Weeks Out

The absolute best thing you can do for yourself is start early. Not "a few days before the movers show up" early—three weeks before your move date early. Your garage has likely become a dumping ground for things you don't use, don't need, and honestly forgot you owned. Moving day is not the time to discover you're paying to transport a rusted-out wheelbarrow and fourteen half-empty bottles of motor oil.

Walk into your garage with a clipboard, your phone camera, and three categories in mind: keep, donate/sell, and dispose of properly. Be honest with yourself. That table saw you haven't plugged in since 2019? If you haven't used it in years, you're not suddenly going to start using it at the new place. The beauty of Denver's active Facebook Marketplace and Craigslist communities is that someone will happily take your unused tools off your hands—sometimes even for decent money.

As you're doing this inventory, take photos of complex tool setups, workbench configurations, and anything mounted on walls. You'll thank yourself later when you're trying to remember how your peg board was organized or which brackets held your bike rack. This is also the perfect time to identify what needs special handling. That air compressor? That's heavy and awkward. The riding lawn mower? That's not going to fit in a standard moving box.

## Dealing With Hazardous Materials the Denver Way

Here's something that catches people off guard every single move: professional moving companies will not—and legally cannot—transport hazardous materials. We're talking about paint, solvents, motor oil, gasoline, propane tanks, fertilizers, pesticides, pool chemicals, aerosol cans, and anything flammable or combustible. If it has a warning label, it's probably not getting on the truck.

Denver has several hazardous waste disposal sites specifically for this purpose. The Denver Household Hazardous Waste Drop-off facility accepts these items for free if you're a Denver resident, and Aurora and other metro cities have similar programs. Check your local city website for drop-off hours and locations. If you're moving in summer, go early in the morning—these places get packed on Saturdays.

For items you absolutely need to keep, like a mostly-full can of expensive deck stain or your collection of specialty automotive fluids, you'll need to transport them yourself in your own vehicle. Use sturdy boxes, pack them upright, secure them so they can't tip over, and keep them in a well-ventilated area of your car. Don't put them in a hot trunk in July and forget about them. One leaked can of paint thinner in your trunk will ruin your entire week.

Propane tanks deserve special mention because they're everywhere in Colorado—attached to grills, heaters, and camp stoves. Most moving companies require tanks to be completely empty before they'll transport them. Some won't transport them at all. Your best bet is to use up the propane before moving day, disconnect the tank, and take it with you in your own vehicle. Many hardware stores will also accept old tanks for recycling or exchange.

## How to Pack Power Tools and Equipment Without Losing Your Mind

Power tools are expensive, and they don't respond well to being tossed around in the back of a moving truck. The good news is that most quality power tools came in cases, and if you still have those cases, packing becomes dramatically easier. If you don't have the original cases—and let's be real, most of us don't—you'll need to get creative.

Start by removing batteries from all cordless tools. This prevents accidental activation during the move and protects battery life. Wrap the batteries separately in bubble wrap or packing paper and keep them together in one clearly labeled box. For corded tools, wrap the cord around the tool body and secure it with a zip tie or piece of tape. Don't let cords dangle—they'll snag on everything and potentially damage the tool or other items.

For larger power tools like table saws, miter saws, and drill presses, you'll want to secure any moving parts. Lock the blade guards, remove detachable fences and accessories, and wrap sharp blades with cardboard secured with tape. If possible, take photos before you start disassembling anything. Power tools often have multiple adjustment knobs and settings, and remembering exactly how everything was positioned becomes impossible after you've packed fifty other items.

Shop vacuums, air compressors, and generators need to be completely drained before moving. Any residual water in a shop vac can slosh around and cause rust. Oil in a generator needs to be drained or you risk it leaking all over your other belongings. If you're not comfortable draining these yourself, most small engine repair shops in the Denver area will do it for a minimal fee, and it's worth it for expensive equipment.

Use heavy-duty moving boxes for power tools—not the flimsy boxes your Amazon deliveries come in. Power tools are heavy and have sharp corners that will punch through cheap cardboard. Fill any empty space in the box with packing paper or bubble wrap so tools can't shift around during transport. Label every box with "HEAVY" and indicate which side should be up. Your back will thank you, and so will the movers.

## Organizing Hand Tools So You Can Actually Find Them Later

Hand tools present a different challenge because you probably have hundreds of them scattered across drawers, pegboards, and that one random bucket in the corner. The worst thing you can do is dump all your screwdrivers, wrenches, and pliers into one big box and call it good. You'll spend weeks at your new place searching for that one specific Allen wrench you need.

Tool boxes and chests can be moved as-is in many cases, but you need to secure the drawers. Use stretch wrap (the industrial plastic wrap movers use) to wrap the entire tool box multiple times, or use strong packing tape to tape drawers shut. Don't rely on whatever locks your tool chest has—moving trucks hit bumps, and drawers can pop open. There's nothing worse than arriving at your new place to find your carefully organized tool drawers have turned into a jumbled mess.

For tools hung on pegboards or wall organizers, you have two options. If the pegboard is small and portable, you can carefully wrap the entire board (with tools still attached) in stretch wrap or moving blankets, keeping everything in place. For larger setups or if you're worried about tools falling off, remove them systematically. This is where those photos you took earlier become invaluable. Take the tools down in sections, wrap them together in groups, and label each bundle with where it came from on the board.

Small parts and hardware are the stuff that disappears into the moving void never to be seen again. Use small plastic containers with lids—empty yogurt containers, plastic jars, or food storage containers work great. Put screws, nails, bolts, and other small items in these containers, label them, and then pack multiple containers together in one larger box. Don't scatter them across multiple boxes or you'll be buying replacement hardware for months.

## Prepping Lawn and Garden Equipment for Transport

Lawn mowers, snow blowers, weed trimmers, and other outdoor power equipment need special attention. The biggest issue is fuel. Gasoline is flammable and can't go on the moving truck. You have a few options: run the equipment until it's completely out of gas, siphon the gas out and store it in approved fuel containers (which you'll transport yourself), or take the equipment to a small engine shop to have it properly drained.

For push mowers, even if you've drained the gas, there's probably still some oil in the crankcase. Tip the mower on its side carefully (check your owner's manual for the correct side—tipping it the wrong way can cause oil to leak into the air filter). Some people drain the oil, others leave it. If you're moving locally within Denver or to a nearby suburb, leaving the oil is usually fine. For longer distances, draining it prevents potential leaks.

Riding lawn mowers and lawn tractors are their own category of challenge. These are heavy, bulky, and difficult to load. If you're hiring professional movers, make sure they know you have a riding mower so they can bring appropriate equipment. Some moving companies charge extra for items like this, and some won't handle them at all, requiring you to rent a trailer or hire a specialized equipment mover.

Snow blowers—which you definitely need if you're staying anywhere in the Denver metro area—should be drained of gas and oil just like mowers. Clean out any leftover snow or ice, let everything dry completely, and wrap protruding parts like chutes and discharge openings with moving blankets or cardboard to prevent damage. If you're moving during winter and know you'll need the snow blower immediately at the new place, consider transporting it yourself rather than putting it on the truck where it might be buried under boxes for days.

Garden tools like shovels, rakes, hoes, and pruning shears can be bundled together. Use bungee cords or rope to tie long-handled tools into a bundle, then wrap the working ends (especially sharp ones like pruners or hedge trimmers) with cardboard secured with tape. Label the bundle clearly and stand it upright in the truck if possible—leaning it against boxes can damage both the tools and the boxes.

## Packing Workshop and Workbench Items Like a Pro

If you have a dedicated workshop space, you know the struggle of having everything organized exactly how you like it, and the thought of packing it all up feels like dismantling your sanctuary. The key is methodical packing with detailed labeling so you can recreate your setup at the new place.

Start with your workbench itself. If it's built-in or custom-made, you'll need to decide whether to disassemble it or leave it for the next homeowner. For portable workbenches, remove everything from the surface and any storage below, then secure any folding or adjustable parts. Wrap the bench in moving blankets if it has a nice work surface you want to protect.

Workshop storage—shelving units, pegboards, parts organizers—should be emptied completely before moving. Even if shelves seem sturdy, the vibration and bumps during transport can send items flying. Those plastic parts organizers with dozens of tiny drawers? Every single drawer needs to be secured or emptied. Use tape or stretch wrap for smaller units, or remove the drawers, bag each one separately, and pack them in boxes.

Measuring and marking tools—levels, tape measures, squares, marking gauges—are precision instruments that don't handle rough treatment well. Pack them with plenty of cushioning, and consider keeping expensive or delicate items like precision levels or digital calipers with you in your car rather than on the moving truck.

Clamps deserve their own mention because if you're a serious DIYer or woodworker, you probably have an unreasonable number of clamps in various sizes. Bundle similar clamps together with bungee cords or zip ties, being careful not to over-tighten and damage the clamps themselves. Wrap bundles in paper or blankets to prevent scratching, and pack them in sturdy boxes or leave them as wrapped bundles that can fit in the gaps between larger items on the truck.

## Handling Specialized Equipment and Machinery

Larger workshop equipment like table saws, band saws, planers, and lathes present serious challenges. These are heavy, expensive, and often need to be bolted down to work properly. If you're moving locally in the Denver area and hiring professional movers, call ahead and ask specifically about moving heavy shop equipment. Many companies have experience with this, but they need to know in advance so they bring the right equipment and enough crew members.

For moving machinery yourself, rent or borrow an appliance dolly rated for the weight of your equipment. Most stationary power tools are way heavier than they look. A cabinet saw can easily weigh 300-500 pounds. Get help—this is not a one-person job, even if you're young and strong. Denver's altitude means you'll get winded faster than you expect, and the last thing you want is a dropped tool or a back injury.

Remove any loose accessories, fences, and attachments before moving. Lower any raising mechanisms to their lowest position for stability. If the equipment has wheels, make sure they're locked or secured. If it doesn't have wheels, consider building a simple rolling platform with casters for easier moving. Many workshop equipment pieces have built-in forklift slots or lifting points—use them if you have access to appropriate equipment.

Welding equipment, air tools, and compressors need hoses disconnected, tanks drained, and regulators protected. Coil hoses neatly, secure them with zip ties, and pack them separately. Gas welding setups cannot be transported by moving companies—you'll need to handle those separately and likely rent a trailer.

3D printers and CNC equipment are sensitive to misalignment and rough handling. If possible, transport these in your own vehicle rather than the moving truck. At minimum, secure all moving parts, remove filament or materials, and pack them in boxes with plenty of cushioning. Take photos of the settings and configurations before unplugging anything.

## The Week Before: Creating Your Garage Moving Plan

One week before your move date, it's time to finalize your garage packing plan. By now, you should have already purged unwanted items and properly disposed of hazardous materials. This week is about methodical packing, labeling, and staging.

Create a staging area in your driveway or a corner of the garage where packed boxes and items ready to move get collected. This prevents the "where did I put that" scramble on moving day. Group similar items together—all garden tools in one area, all power tools in another, automotive supplies separate from workshop items.

Label boxes extensively. Don't just write "garage" on every box—that's useless when you're trying to find your drill bits in a sea of identical boxes. Instead, use labels like "Garage - Power Tools - Drills & Drivers," "Garage - Hand Tools - Wrenches & Sockets," or "Garage - Garden - Hand Tools." Include a brief list of major contents on the side of the box. Yes, it takes extra time. Yes, you'll be grateful you did it.

For items that can't go in boxes—bicycles, ladders, wheelbarrows, larger equipment—group them together and make sure they're clean and ready to load. Moving companies often load these last and unload them first, so they don't crush your boxes of breakable items.

If you're using a professional moving company like those serving Denver and the surrounding communities, walk through your garage with them during the estimate. Point out heavy items, ask about any concerns, and clarify what you're packing versus what you want them to handle. Good communication prevents surprises on moving day.

Make a "load last, unpack first" box of essential garage items you'll need immediately at the new place. This might include basic hand tools, a flashlight, extension cords, trash bags, cleaning supplies, and anything else you'll need for quick fixes or organizing at the new place. Keep this box with you or clearly label it so it doesn't get buried in the truck.

## Moving Day: Loading and Unloading the Garage Items

Moving day has arrived, and your garage is packed and ready. If you've hired professional movers, they'll have a system for loading. Generally, heavy items like equipment and machinery go in first near the front of the truck, followed by boxes, with lighter items and things that can go on top loaded last.

Communicate clearly about what's what. If something is particularly heavy or awkward, give the movers a heads up. If a box contains especially valuable tools, mention it. Professional movers handle this stuff every day, but they appreciate knowing about special concerns.

For the items you're transporting yourself—hazardous materials, particularly valuable tools, or things that just make you nervous on a truck—load them securely in your vehicle. Don't overload your car to the point where you can't see out the windows or where items could slide forward and hit you in an accident.

At the new place, decide before the truck arrives where everything garage-related is going. If you have a new garage, direct everything there. If you're moving to a place without garage space, you might need to designate a storage area, basement space, or even rent a storage unit temporarily. Having a plan prevents the "just set it anywhere" approach that leads to chaos.

When unloading, don't try to organize everything immediately. The goal on moving day is to get everything off the truck and into the general vicinity of where it belongs. Deep organization can happen over the next few weeks. That said, if you have heavy equipment or machinery, position it roughly where it will eventually go—you don't want to move a 400-pound table saw twice.

## Setting Up Your Garage at the New Place

Once you're in your new Denver-area home, whether it's in Aurora, Littleton, or anywhere else in the metro, resist the urge to unpack your garage first. The living spaces should take priority—you need places to sleep and eat before you need access to your socket set. That said, unpacking essentials early is smart.

Start with basic organization. If your new garage has built-in storage, take advantage of it. If it doesn't, this might be the time to install some shelving or pegboard before you fill the space with stuff. It's much easier to install shelves in an empty garage than in one already full of boxes.

As you unpack tool boxes and equipment, check that everything survived the move intact. Power tools should be tested to ensure they work. Check that blades and bits are still sharp and undamaged. If something broke during the move, document it immediately—especially if you purchased moving insurance.

Reorganize with your new space in mind. What worked in your old garage might not work in your new one. Maybe you have more space now and can spread out. Maybe you have less space and need to be more strategic about storage. Take the opportunity to improve your organization system.

Don't rush reassembly of complex equipment or machinery. Take your time releveling tools, recalibrating settings, and ensuring everything is properly secured before you start using them. A table saw that isn't level won't cut accurately, and a drill press that's not secured will vibrate excessively.

Finally, update any documentation. If you registered tools or equipment with manufacturers, update your address. If you have homeowners insurance that specifically covers valuable tools or equipment, notify your insurance company about the move.

## When to Call in Professional Help

While packing and moving garage items yourself is certainly possible, there are situations where professional help makes sense. If you have an extensive workshop with expensive equipment, the peace of mind that comes with professional movers who have insurance and experience handling these items can be worth the cost. Heavy machinery, in particular, benefits from professional moving equipment and expertise.

The Denver metro area has numerous moving companies experienced with garage and workshop moves. When getting quotes, be specific about what you have. Photos help immensely. Ask about experience with workshop equipment, whether they charge extra for heavy items, and what their insurance covers. If you're working with a company that handles local moves throughout Denver and surrounding areas, they'll have seen it all before and can offer guidance on best practices.

For particularly valuable or antique tools, consider specialized moving services or even transporting them yourself. That restored hand plane collection or vintage power tools might be irreplaceable, and the extra care of personal transport could be warranted.

Storage solutions during a move can also be invaluable. If there's a gap between when you move out and when you move in, or if your new garage isn't ready yet, short-term storage keeps your tools safe and out of the way. Climate-controlled storage is worth considering for tools susceptible to rust in Denver's variable humidity and temperature conditions.

Remember that preparing your garage for a move is about more than just getting stuff from point A to point B. It's about protecting your investment in tools and equipment, disposing of hazardous materials responsibly, and setting yourself up for success in your new workspace. Take it step by step, start early, and don't hesitate to ask for help when you need it. Your future self—standing in your organized new garage with every tool in its place—will thank you for the effort you put in now.

Whether you're moving across Denver, to a nearby suburb, or making a longer distance move out of state, the principles of garage preparation remain the same: sort ruthlessly, pack methodically, label extensively, and handle heavy items with care. With proper planning and execution, your garage move can be one of the smoother aspects of your relocation rather than the nightmare it sometimes becomes when left to the last minute.
